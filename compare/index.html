First decision

Three shapes of system. Pick the shape before the brand.

Almost every bad software purchase in this trade is a shape error, not a brand error — buying an all-in-one to solve one problem, or bolting a fourth specialist tool onto three you already re-key data between.

1. Rota-only

RotaCloud · Deputy · Rotaready · Planday · Bizimply

Scheduling, and usually clock-in, holidays and shift swaps. Priced per head almost without exception, which is the thing to understand about this shape: the bill grows every time you hire. Some charge time-and-attendance as an extra on top.

Buy this if your till is fine and the rota is what eats your week. It is the cheapest way to fix one specific problem, and the specialists are genuinely good at it.

The catch: it does not know what you took. Labour cost percentage needs a sales figure, so either you type takings in by hand every day or the number is a guess. And payroll is an export, not a run.

2. Till-only

Square · Lightspeed · Toast · Tabology · SumUp POS Pro · ICRTouch · Akru

Point of sale, and increasingly kitchen display and stock. Priced per venue or per terminal — and which of those it is matters more than the headline. Several of these make most of their money on card processing rather than software, which is why some of them are cheap or free.

Buy this if you are opening, or your till is genuinely the problem. You cannot trade without one, so it is the non-negotiable purchase.

The catch: of the eleven till systems we compared, only two advertise compliance records at all, and most hand rota and payroll to a third party. You will be buying something else as well.

3. All-in-one

Kobas · HospitalityApp · parts of Zonal and Access at estate scale

Till, rota, clock-in, payroll, stock and compliance from one system. Priced per venue, which is the structural argument for it: the second till and the twenty-sixth staff member do not add to the bill.

Buy this if the joins are what is costing you — re-keying hours into payroll, not knowing last week's labour percentage without building a spreadsheet, compliance records in a folder nobody opens.

The catch: if you only need one thing, you are paying for eleven. A specialist will usually beat an all-in-one at any single job. And integration risk concentrates: one vendor's outage is now all of your systems.

Do it for your own pub

The arithmetic, so you can check ours.

Three sums. Do them before you shortlist anything, because they usually reorder the shortlist.

  1. Card processing, per year. monthly card turnover × 12 × the rate, plus (turnover ÷ average transaction) × any per-transaction fee. Take that figure to every vendor and ask them to beat it, or to confirm you can keep your existing deal.
  2. Software, per year, for your device count. Not the headline. (base + per-terminal fee × extra devices + per-head fee × chargeable staff) × 12. Count the kitchen screen and any handhelds as devices, because several vendors do.
  3. Cost of leaving in month six. Ask for it in writing. On a 36-month contract at £99 a month, an accelerated exit clause makes that £2,970 payable immediately — more than a year of software.

Then add hardware once. A one-bar setup is roughly £575–£865 ex VAT, and two bars with a kitchen printer about £1,476–£2,073 ex VAT. It is a real cost but it is the smallest of the four and it is paid once.

Common questions

Choosing a shape.

Do I need a rota system, a till, or an all-in-one?

Start from what you are actually short of. If your rota eats your week and the till works fine, buy a rota tool and keep the till. If you are opening and have nothing, buy the till first — you cannot trade without one. Buy an all-in-one when the joins start costing money: re-keying hours into payroll, not knowing last week's labour percentage without a spreadsheet, compliance in a folder nobody opens. The mistake is buying an all-in-one to solve one problem and paying for eleven modules you never open.

What actually costs the most in year one?

Card processing, and it is not close. For a pub taking £45,000 a month, the spread across every software option we could price is about £1,212 a year. The spread between a 1.75% card rate and a 1.2% one is £2,970 — roughly two and a half times the software decision. If a till locks you to its own processor, that lock is worth more than the monthly fee.

Is an all-in-one always cheaper?

No. If you only need one thing, a specialist is usually cheaper and often better at it. All-in-one wins on two specific axes: you stop paying per-head or per-terminal fees on the second product, and you stop re-keying between systems. In our worked example a till plus a per-head rota tool is about £1,548 a year against £1,188 for an all-in-one — but that gap exists only because the per-head fee scales with your team and the per-venue one does not.

What is a per-terminal fee and why does it matter?

Some vendors charge once per venue; others charge per till, per kitchen screen and sometimes per handheld. Published figures in this market run from about £15 to £39 a month per extra device. A two-bar pub with a kitchen screen has three screens, so the second and third can add several hundred pounds a year to an otherwise identical setup. It is the question most likely to reorder your shortlist, and it is almost never on the pricing page in plain terms.

How do I compare systems that will not publish a price?

Ask for the total contract value rather than the monthly figure — monthly fee times the full term, plus setup, plus any support plan on its own separate clock. Then ask what you pay to leave in month six. Vendors that do not publish pricing are not being disorganised; it is a commercial decision, and it means the first number you see will have been chosen for you after they know your turnover.

Where do these figures come from?

Vendors' own live UK pricing pages, read on 25 August 2026, plus their published contracts where they have one. Where a vendor publishes nothing, the table says “not published” rather than repeating a review site's number.

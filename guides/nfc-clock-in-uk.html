What it is

Near-field, actually near-field.

NFC (Near-Field Communication) is the same technology that runs contactless card payment. A phone held within 2-4cm of an NFC tag reads its unique ID (UID) in a fraction of a second. Apple and Google Wallet use it; TfL uses it; the tap-to-open in your hotel room uses it. It's ubiquitous, cheap and boring — three good qualities for a payroll input.

  • Physical presence. The staff member's phone has to be at the tag — usually a sticker on the till or the staff entrance. A photo can be sent from anywhere; a PIN can be typed from anywhere; a tag has to be tapped in the venue.
  • Speed. Under two seconds from unlocked phone in hand to punch recorded. Fast enough that staff actually use it instead of tapping "Confirmed" through a screen sequence.
  • No shared hardware. Staff use their own phones. You provide the tag and the app; they provide the reader.
How the registry works

Tags register by UID, label by hand.

  • Buy NFC tags — NTAG213 or NTAG215 stickers, usually 20-50p each in packs of ten from Amazon or a specialist supplier.
  • In the app, open Time & attendance → NFC tags → Register a new tag; tap the tag with your phone; the UID (a 14-character hex string) auto-fills.
  • Give it a label a human can read: "Bar till", "Kitchen door", "Cellar top". This is what shows up in the audit log.
  • Stick the tag where it belongs. Test with one clock-in.
  • Remove a lost tag from the registry to invalidate it instantly; buy a replacement, register the new UID, stick it in place.
Combining methods

NFC + GPS geofence closes buddy-punching.

The three HospitalityApp clock-in methods (GPS geofence, QR code, NFC tag) are combinable. Requiring NFC and a GPS check within the venue's geofence means the punch only lands if the phone is holding a tag at the venue — a story that's much harder to fake than either alone.

Practical setup for a pub:

  • NFC tag on the till (or the staff entrance if the till is in a public spot).
  • GPS geofence: 50m radius around the venue postcode.
  • Require both — the “also require GPS” toggle sits next to the method list in settings.

Cost: about £5 in tags. Time to set up: 15 minutes. Buddy-punching risk after that: effectively zero, because the shared-phone attack requires being physically present at the venue, at the right time, with the right person's logged-in device — at which point the staff member might as well come to work.

Overnight and edge cases

The rules the software should already know.

  • Overnight-safe. A shift crossing midnight is one session; a tap-in at 20:00 and tap-out at 02:30 records as 6.5 hours, not two shifts.
  • Clock-out grace window. A closer finishing 20 minutes past the scheduled end isn't punished. Configurable per venue.
  • Auto-close sweep. A forgotten clock-out is closed at a sensible end and flagged for manager review — never a 30-hour shift on payroll.
  • Future-punch clamp. A device with a wrong clock can't write a punch into the future.
  • Discrepancies queue. Every no-show, still-clocked-in and flagged session lands in one manager screen with one-tap resolutions — Pay as planned, Close out at 23:15, Mark as no-show, Confirm as-is, Dismiss.
The audit trail

Immutable by design.

The clock model is append-only. Punches are individual events; sessions are derived from them by a database trigger. You can correct a session (add a missing clock-out, close a still-open one, mark a no-show) but you can't rewrite the underlying punches — every correction is recorded as a new event with an audit note against the manager who made it.

That matters if a pay dispute goes to a tribunal, or a payroll audit questions a specific week: the record is intact and dated, and the reason for every correction is on the record next to it.

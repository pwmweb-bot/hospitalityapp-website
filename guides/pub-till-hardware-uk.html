Guide · Till hardware · August 2026

What till hardware a pub actually needs — and what you already own.

Everyone selling a till system wants to sell you a tablet. This is the buying order, the real prices with their VAT basis, the two technical facts that cost people the most money, and the contract questions that matter more than any of the hardware.

The short answer

Wet-led bar, no food, one serving point: if you already own a tablet, you need a stand and nothing else. About £33 ex VAT. Take card on Tap to Pay with a phone you already have. No printer means no cash drawer, and that is a legitimate way to run a card-led bar.

A proper one-bar setup — tablet, stand, network receipt printer, cash drawer, card reader, rolls — lands at £575–£865 ex VAT (£690–£1,038 inc VAT).

Two bars and a kitchen, including an impact printer on the pass and a spare tablet on the shelf: £1,476–£2,073 ex VAT (£1,771–£2,488 inc VAT).

If someone is quoting you thousands for a single-bar pub, they are selling you hardware you do not need in year one.

Before you spend anything

Three questions for your till provider, in writing.

The reason people waste money on till hardware is buying order. They buy the printer before asking the software what it can talk to, and the stand before choosing the tablet. These three answers decide your printer brand, your card terminal and your five-year cost.

1. Is the licence charged per location or per device? Does a kitchen display, a handheld, a kiosk and a back-office login each count as a chargeable device? What does a fifth till cost in year two?

2. My till is a tablet running in a browser. How do you print — Epson ePOS-Print, Star CloudPRNT, or through your own app? This one picks your printer brand for you. Ask it before you buy a printer, not after.

3. Which card terminals do you integrate with, and can I keep my own acquirer without paying you a premium for it?

VerdictGet all three in an email. A salesperson's verbal yes is worth nothing in month eight, and every one of these has a documented cost attached in this market.
The mistake that costs the most

Why a USB printer will not work with your tablet.

There is one mistake in this whole category that costs more than all the others put together, and it is a £24 saving.

Go to any UK trade supplier and look up the Epson TM-T20IV. There are two of them on the page. One is £119.36 ex VAT. One is £143.39 ex VAT. Same printer, same speed, same paper, near-identical photograph. The cheap one is USB and serial. The dearer one adds Ethernet.

If your till is an iPad or an Android tablet, the cheap one is a paperweight.

A Windows PC has a driver system: you install a small piece of software that knows how to speak to that specific printer, and everything on the computer can then print to it. An iPad has nothing of the kind. There is no printer driver system and no USB printing class. Plug a USB receipt printer into an iPad and precisely nothing happens — no error, no prompt, no printer. It is not a cable problem, and no adapter fixes it.

What a tablet can do is talk over a network. A LAN printer has its own address on your pub's network, exactly like a wireless office printer. Anything on that network can send it a job: the iPad at the bar, a second iPad you add next summer, the manager's laptop. Nothing is physically married to anything. Compare USB, where the printer belongs to one device forever, or Bluetooth, where it is paired to one device at a time and you will spend your life re-pairing it.

The bit nobody explains

“LAN printer” on its own is not quite enough if your till runs inside a web browser rather than as an installed app. A web page is not permitted to open a raw network socket. It cannot squirt data at the printer's port 9100 the way an installed application can. That is a deliberate security boundary in every browser on earth, and no amount of configuration removes it.

There are exactly three ways round it, and the answer picks your brand for you:

  1. Epson ePOS-Print. The browser sends the job to the printer's IP address as an ordinary HTTP request, which the printer understands natively. It also lets the till check paper-out and cover-open before sending. Supported across the current Epson TM range.
  2. Star CloudPRNT. The printer phones your till provider's server every few seconds asking for jobs and pulls them down. Two useful consequences: the ordering device does not need to be on the same network, and you never need to know the printer's IP address. Tidiest option for online orders landing in your kitchen from off-site.
  3. A native till app on the tablet. An installed app is not a web page and is not bound by the browser's rules, so it can open a socket to port 9100 and speak ESC/POS directly — the same conversation a Windows till has always had, just over the network instead of a cable. HospitalityApp prints this way, over your own LAN to port 9100.
VerdictIf your till is a tablet, the printer needs Ethernet. If your till is a Windows PC, buy the USB one and keep the £24. Either way, ask the printing question before you order.
The other one everyone gets wrong

Your cash drawer does not plug into your tablet.

There is no cable from the iPad to the cash drawer. No Bluetooth pairing, no app, no setting. The drawer plugs into the back of your receipt printer, into a small telephone-style socket labelled DK — for “drawer kick”.

The chain runs: tablet → printer → drawer.

Here is what actually happens when a customer pays cash. Your bartender taps Cash on the tablet. The till software sends the printer two things: the text of the receipt, and an ESC/POS drawer-kick command. The printer prints the receipt, then sends a short 24-volt pulse down the DK cable. That pulse energises a solenoid inside the drawer, which flicks the latch, and a spring shoves the drawer out at the person standing in front of it.

The drawer has no brain. It has no power supply of its own; it draws its 24V from the printer's mains adaptor, through pin 4 of a six-pin modular connector. The trade calls that connector RJ11 on one listing and RJ12 on the next — in this context they mean the same socket, so do not let it confuse you.

Four things follow, and each saves someone money:

  • You do not need a separate drawer controller. The printer is the drawer controller. Anyone quoting an interface box on top of a standard printer-driven drawer is padding the invoice.
  • You cannot have a self-opening drawer without a printer. If you have sensibly decided to skip the receipt printer, a plain cash drawer will never open by itself. Buy a lockable point-of-sale safe instead — a Safescan 4100 is around £49 ex VAT. The one genuine exception is a small drawer-controller box such as the Star mC-Connect, around £105 ex VAT, which gives the drawer its own USB-C, Ethernet or Bluetooth so a tablet can fire it directly — but your till software has to support it explicitly, so ask first. The cheap “RJ11-to-USB cash drawer trigger” dongles are not an alternative; they present as a virtual serial port with Windows, Mac and Linux drivers only.
  • Buy the printer first, then the drawer. The drawer is an accessory of the printer, not of the tablet. A supplier selling you a drawer before establishing which printer you are on is not thinking about your setup.
  • Check the voltage and the cable. 24V is the UK norm across Epson, Star, Bixolon, Citizen and Zebra. A 12V drawer on a 24V port burns out its solenoid. And confirm the cable suits your printer brand — Star drawers ship a near-universal lead, while APG drawers using the 320 interface need a brand-matched adapter. That £8 part is the single most common reason a brand-new drawer sits there doing nothing.

One last thing that saves a purchase: pins 2 and 5 are drawer one and drawer two, so a single printer can drive two drawers. If you have a two-server bar sharing one printer, you need a splitter cable, not a second printer.

Ask your till provider: “Does your app actually send the drawer-kick command on a cash sale, and can a manager open the drawer for a no-sale from the app?”

If the answer to the first is no, the hardware is irrelevant. Nothing will open.

If you serve food

The bar wants thermal. The kitchen wants the opposite.

This sounds like fussiness and it is not.

A thermal printer has no ink and no ribbon. The paper is coated with a colourless dye and an acid developer, and the print head heats them until they react and go black. That is why the only running cost is the roll, why it is fast, and why it is silent.

The problem is that the reaction is not permanent and it is not fussy about where the heat comes from. Heat, steam, grease, oil and sunlight all degrade it or set it off. A thermal receipt kept cool and dry stays readable for months. A thermal ticket hanging on a rail above a grill or a fryer will grey out, blur, or go uniformly black within minutes.

An impact printer — a dot-matrix, like an old office printer — hammers a nine-pin head through an inked ribbon onto ordinary plain paper. Physical ink on plain paper does not care about heat or grease at all.

The second reason chefs prefer them is noise. Epson's own product page for the TM-U220 sells the “distinctive printing sound” as a feature, because it draws attention to an incoming order in a loud kitchen. A silent thermal printer gets ignored, which is a service failure disguised as a hardware choice.

Two practical consequences:

  • An impact printer takes plain (bond) rolls, not thermal rolls. They look nearly identical on a stockroom shelf. Load a thermal roll into your kitchen printer and you get faint, patchy, near-illegible tickets and a member of staff who will confidently report that the printer is broken. Store them separately and write on the boxes.
  • Buy the black/red ribbon (ERC-38BR), not the black-only (ERC-38B). It is a couple of pounds. Two-colour printing lets you flag allergens and modifiers in red on the ticket — a genuine safety benefit on a busy pass for the price of a packet of crisps.
VerdictThermal behind the bar, impact on the pass. If you are food-led, buy the kitchen printer first — most people buy the bar printer first and then discover the useful one was the one in the kitchen.
Your escape hatch

ESC/POS: why it matters that your printer is boring.

ESC/POS stands for “Epson Standard Code for Point of Sale”. It is the language a till uses to tell a printer what to do — this line bold, this line centred, cut the paper here, open the drawer now. Epson invented it, it became the de facto industry standard, and it is now why almost any till software can drive almost any receipt printer.

That is your escape hatch, and it is the most valuable thing on the spec sheet. If you change till providers in three years, an ESC/POS printer will almost certainly still work with the new one. A printer that only speaks its manufacturer's proprietary protocol, or hardware locked to one vendor's platform, is scrap the day you leave.

Two honest caveats. It is a de facto standard, not a policed one: every manufacturer implements it slightly differently, so “ESC/POS compatible” on a £45 marketplace listing is a claim rather than a certification. Usually the basics work — print, cut, kick the drawer — and the edges are where it falls over: logos, barcodes, character sets, the pound sign. And Star printers speak their own StarPRNT by default, offering ESC/POS as an emulation mode you may need to switch on during setup. If your till software says “ESC/POS only” and you have bought a Star, that setting is the first thing to check before deciding the printer is faulty.

Already have kit?

Will my current hardware work?

Ten questions, each answerable in under a minute standing behind your own bar.

1. Is your tablet an iPad 9th generation or newer, or an Android tablet still getting security updates?

Yes — it will run a browser-based till and has years of life. The iPad model is in Settings → General → About.

No — it is finished as a till, even if it still switches on. An iPad Air 2 stops at iPadOS 15, a 5th gen at 16, 6th at 17, 7th at 18. And iPadOS 27 drops the iPad 8th gen, Air 3, mini 5 and the 2018 Pros — all of which run today. A frozen browser eventually stops working with cloud software.

2. Does your tablet charge with USB-C, or Lightning?

USB-C — the modern stands fit, including the Bouncepad Core range, which is built around a centred USB-C port.

Lightning (iPad 9th gen and older) — you are limited to enclosure-style stands and universal stands like the Heckler @Rest. Do not order a Core.

3. Does your receipt printer have an Ethernet socket?

Yes — it will work with a tablet till.

USB only — it will not. Not with an adapter, not with a hub. Fine on a Windows PC, useless on an iPad.

4. Is your receipt paper 80mm or 58mm wide?

80mm — correct for a bar. About 42–48 characters a line: item, quantity and price without wrapping, and a legible kitchen docket with modifiers.

58mm — about 32 characters. Item names wrap onto second lines and food dockets get genuinely hard to read across a hot pass. An 80mm printer can also print 58mm; a 58mm printer can never print wide. This is a one-way door.

5. Is your cash drawer 24V, and does its cable match your printer brand?

Yes to both — keep it. A working drawer plus a £5–£10 cable is a much better buy than a new drawer.

12V — do not plug it into a 24V printer port. You will burn out the solenoid.

Unsure about the cable? Star drawers ship a near-universal RJ11 lead. APG drawers using the 320 MultiPRO interface need a brand-matched adapter. That £8 part is the commonest reason a brand-new drawer does not open.

6. Is your kitchen printer thermal or impact?

Impact (has a ribbon cartridge, is noisy) — correct. Keep it.

Thermal (no ribbon, silent) — wrong tool. It will grey out, blur or blacken above a grill, and chefs will ignore it because it makes no noise.

7. Can you run a network cable to each printer position?

Yes — do it. Wireless costs about £50 more per printer and introduces the worst failure mode in the category: the silent one, where the order rings through fine and no docket ever appears.

No (garden bar, listed building) — then and only then pay for the wireless model.

8. Does your card terminal receive the amount from the till automatically, or does someone type it in?

Automatically — it is integrated. Find out which EPOS systems it works with before you change till software, because that integration is worth more than the box.

Typed in — it is standalone. That is the normal state of affairs for most independent pubs, and it is also what you would have with us. We say so plainly below.

9. Is your printer's IP address reserved on your router?

Yes — nothing to do.

No or don't know — do it now. By default your router hands out addresses that change on reboot, and when the printer's address moves, the till loses it. It is a DHCP reservation in the router admin page. Five minutes now saves a Friday night later.

10. Do you have mains power at each till point?

Yes — plan a permanent cable and a stand with internal routing.

No — solve that before you buy anything else. A till that dies at half nine on a Saturday is worse than no till, and swapping tablets at half time is not a plan.

Don't buy these

What you can skip.

  • A barcode scanner. A pub does not need one. If you sell packaged goods in volume it earns its place; otherwise it is a solved problem you do not have. One technical note if you do buy: a 1D laser scanner physically cannot read a barcode off a phone screen, because a screen emits light rather than reflecting it. If you plan to scan vouchers or loyalty codes from phones, you need a 2D imager.
  • A rugged tablet. A £33 stand and a normal tablet is the better bet. Rugged kit is for warehouses.
  • 256GB of storage. A till app and a menu are small. Buy the base storage and spend the difference on a spare tablet.
  • Wireless printers, unless you genuinely cannot run a cable. About £50 more per printer for a worse failure mode.
  • A separate drawer controller on top of a printer-driven drawer. The printer already is one.
  • A kitchen display screen on day one. Buy the impact printer. Add a screen in six months if the ticket rail actually becomes unmanageable — most single-kitchen pubs find it does not.
The expensive part

The hardware is the cheap bit. The contract is where the money is.

A one-bar hardware setup is £600–£900 ex VAT, once. A till contract is a monthly figure times a term you probably have not multiplied out, plus a card contract with its own end date, plus possibly a hardware lease with a finance company you have never heard of.

And here is the thing nobody says out loud: as a business, you have no cooling-off period. The 14-day cancellation right you have as a consumer does not apply. Consumer Credit Act protections are disapplied for agreements entered into wholly or predominantly for business purposes, and the paperwork will contain a business-purpose declaration for you to sign, which creates a legal presumption to exactly that effect. A five-year lease signed on the bar at four o'clock on a Tuesday, after a forty-minute pitch, is binding at 4:01. That is precisely why these are sold face to face, at speed, with a same-day incentive.

Print this and take it to the meeting.

Licensing — per till or per pub?

  • “Quote me in writing for four till licences, one kitchen display and one office login, per month, ex VAT.”
  • “Is the licence per location or per device? Do a kitchen display, a handheld and a back-office login each count as chargeable?”
  • “What does a fifth till cost in year two?”

Why: extra registers are published at £39 a month each by some vendors; Epos Now's own terms price standard devices at £15 a month each; Square's Plus plan is per location with unlimited devices. A four-till pub can face a four-figure annual difference on identical hardware.

Term and exit

  • “What is the total contract value — monthly fee times the full term?”
  • “What exactly do I pay if I cancel in month six? Read me the termination clause.”

Why: the exit charge is commonly the entire remaining balance, accelerated. Epos Now's terms specify “a sum representing the aggregate of the remaining monthly payments”. On a 36-month deal at £99 a month cancelled at month six, that is £2,970 ex VAT due immediately.

Leasing — who is actually lending you the money?

  • “Is this a lease with a third-party finance company? Name them and send me their agreement before I sign anything.”
  • “Is it cancellable at any point — yes or no? If not, what is the settlement figure at month 12, 24 and 36?”
  • “If your company stops trading, am I still obliged to pay the funder every remaining rental?”
  • “Who owns the hardware at the end? Are you asking me for a personal guarantee as a director?”

Why: your contract with the funder is legally separate from your contract with the supplier. The funder's obligation is to fund, not to make the system work. A documented UK case: a publican signed a five-year lease for one till, printer and drawer at 19 quarterly payments of £342.75 + VAT. Two years later the supplier said the software needed upgrading and put them on a second lease, with a different funder, for the same physical hardware — 19 more quarterly payments of £437.14 + VAT. Both ran at once. Roughly £15,600 ex VAT for one till.

Auto-renewal, and the support plan's separate clock

  • “What is the end date, and the last date I can serve notice to stop it renewing? Put both on the order confirmation.”
  • “Is support a separate agreement from the software licence? Give me both renewal dates and both notice periods.”

Why: notice windows are commonly 90–180 days before the end date, and nobody diarises a date three years out during a busy trading week. The support plan rolls on its own clock, is often taken from a stored card rather than invoiced, and can auto-renew for another year after you have cancelled the software.

Card processing — the biggest recurring number

  • “Can I use my existing acquirer with this till? If I do, do you charge me any fee or premium for it?”
  • “Is the terminal locked to one processor at firmware level?”
  • “Send me every recurring line on the merchant statement, not just the rate — PCI fee, PCI non-compliance fee, minimum monthly service charge, authorisation fee, terminal rental, gateway fee.”

Why: two shapes of lock-in. Hard — some terminals are welded to one acquirer. Soft — Epos Now's terms give you 60 days to sign with one of their payment providers, after which you default to a “Non-Integrated Payment Premium” of £29 a month per Pro solution. That is £348 a year for the offence of keeping your own bank.

Ownership, setup, and the tied-pub question

  • “On this £0-upfront deal, who holds legal title to the hardware — me or you? Put it in the order confirmation.”
  • “How many days do I have to return it after termination, and what exact sum do you invoice if I don't?”
  • “Is this standard off-the-shelf kit or proprietary? If I leave in three years, can this terminal run any other EPOS software?”
  • “One written quote including menu and modifier build, VAT setup, table plan, data migration, installation, cabling and training — with the hours included and the rate beyond that.”
  • If you are a tied tenant, ask the pubco before any supplier: “Does my tenancy require a specified EPOS system, and is there a mandatory monthly EPOS charge?” Some tenants report restrictions — check your own agreement rather than assuming.

Why: “free” hardware is a bailment, not a gift. Epos Now's terms set the hardware cost at £1,500 or £1,200 depending on plan, invoiceable if it is not returned. Proprietary terminals have no resale value the day you switch. And implementation is routinely a four-figure one-off that never appears on the pricing page.

Common questions

What publicans ask.

Do I actually need a receipt printer in a pub?

If you are wet-led with no kitchen, no table service and no second serving point — honestly, no, not on day one. Your card machine prints its own card receipt regardless, most drinkers do not ask for a till receipt, and the cash-up can be read on screen. You need one the moment you serve food, add a second bar, do table service, or take online orders that must reach the kitchen. And if you are food-led, buy the kitchen printer first and the bar printer second.

Can I use my old iPad as a till?

Check which one it is in Settings → General → About. An iPad Air 2, 5th, 6th or 7th generation is genuinely finished — frozen at iPadOS 15, 16, 17 or 18, with a browser that cloud till software will eventually refuse to run. An iPad 8th gen, Air 3 or mini 5 works today but is dropped by iPadOS 27. An iPad 9th generation or newer is a safe base. Also check the port: 9th gen and older are Lightning, which rules out several modern stands. “Still switches on” is not the same as “still works”.

Why won't my cash drawer open?

Almost always one of four things, in this order. One: it is not plugged into the receipt printer — the drawer connects to the printer's DK port, never to the tablet. Two: the cable is wrong for your printer brand. Three: your till software is not sending the drawer-kick command at all, in which case no hardware will help. Four: voltage — 24V is the UK norm, and a 24V drawer on a 12V port will not open. A drawer with a dead printer still opens with the manual key override.

Do I have to buy new software licences for a second bar?

You have to buy the hardware — tablet, stand and a printer for that serving point. Whether you pay again for the software depends entirely on your provider, and it is the question worth asking in writing. Some vendors bill per device, with published figures around £15–£39 per month per extra terminal; others bill per location with unlimited devices. On a four-till pub that runs into four figures a year on identical hardware. HospitalityApp has no per-terminal fee.

For a wet-led bar with a tablet you already own: a universal stand at about £33 ex VAT and Tap to Pay on an iPhone XS or newer. Under £40 ex VAT.
